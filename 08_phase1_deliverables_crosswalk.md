## 1. Sources

| Source | What it says that matters for Phase I |

| **Course brief** (`Course Project - inclass.pdf`) | Phase I = "User Studies to evolve Requirements, Design and Workflows". Deliverables: **User Study Evaluation reports, Requirements, Design Prototypes**. Teams must do design research before prototypes, collect **qualitative and quantitative** data, using primary methods (questionnaires, focus groups, contextual interviews) and secondary methods (comparative analyses of existing applications). Poster 1 compiles usability studies and design themes. The final video Part A asks: how did you design the studies, how did they improve the tool, what are your design themes and methodology |

| **Lecture 2** | Usable security study challenges (ecological validity, realistic sense of risk, incentives, not biasing participants). The 8-step study process including piloting and, where needed, ethics approval |
| **Lecture 3** | Study steps (goals, protocol and tasks, pilot, conduct, analyse). Participant guidance (4 to 5 users find about 80% of issues). **Informed consent elements** and the lighter study information sheet. Task design (scenario, goal, end criterion, time limit). Data: think-aloud, screencast, questionnaires, interviews. **Critical incident** reports (problem statement, user goal, immediate intention, possible causes) and consolidation. Prototype types and fidelity; horizontal and vertical; low-fidelity paper prototyping with user, "computer" and observer |
| **Lecture 4** | Mental models (user, system, conceptual, designer); how users acquire them; design around existing models |
| **Assignment 1 brief** (your precedent) | A usability study report with: project details; link to a low-fidelity prototype; link to a protocol design or workflow flowchart (the steps a user follows for a task); compilation of primary and secondary research and results; insights drawn (mind maps, SWOT or similar boards) |
| **Outside practice** | A usability report usually has: executive summary (what we tested, what we found, what to do), goals, methodology, participants, findings with frequency, impact and severity (0 to 4), recommendations ordered by severity, and an appendix ([Xtensio](https://xtensio.com/how-to-write-a-usability-testing-report/), [Contentsquare](https://contentsquare.com/guides/usability-testing/report/), [Medium guide](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/usability-test-report-a-guide-to-make-and-present-4f560c5074de)). Unmoderated remote tests work best with specific, actionable tasks, 15 to 30 minutes at most, short post-task questions and a mix of completion, time and error data with comments ([NN/g on remote tests](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/remote-usability-tests/), [UXArmy](https://uxarmy.com/blog/writing-tasks-in-unmoderated-usability-testing/)) |

## 3. What this changes from the earlier plan

1. The **website is the submission**, with a PDF export of key pages as backup. A reviewer can see every deliverable in one place.
2. The **survey becomes a remote usability test** run on classmates: scenario tasks with end criteria and time on task measured automatically, post-task ratings, critical-incident prompts and open questions that accept voice or typing. This matches Lecture 3 and outside practice for unmoderated tests.
3. Classmates are told **what they get** (a participation receipt that counts toward the 1% per five studies) and **what we ask of them** (about 12 minutes), with consent in plain words.
4. Severity ratings (0 to 4) are added to critical incidents (outside practice).
5. We still need **live sessions** (5 watched think-aloud sessions) for depth. The website is the stimulus for those sessions too.
