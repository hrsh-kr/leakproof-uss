# C2 (P2 + D2): wrapped shares on the server, browser unwrap

## (a) Strongest case
- **Every share has an owner and a place to live.** Each custodian enrols a keypair in the browser (WebCrypto ECDH/ECDSA P-256). The private half is stored wrapped under a passphrase-derived key. At sealing the server wraps each Shamir share to that custodian's public key and stores only ciphertext. The DB, backups and the VM operator see no usable share. A lone tester has something to click: the share exists, in the app.
- **Approval is cryptographic, not a click.** Release needs the share itself, unwrapped in the custodian's browser. The browser also signs `examId | centre | requestId | nonce | expiry` with the custodian's key. The server checks the signature against the enrolled public key and the share against its stored commitment. No share means no key. A stolen session cannot approve, because the session never holds the private key.
- **It is buildable and testable.** It reuses `makeShares`/`combine` and WebCrypto and adds no new primitive. One test per attack: replay, wrong custodian, expired, duplicate share, 2 of 5.

## (b) Two best attacks on C1 (H1 + S3)
1. **C1 contradicts itself.** H1 bans typed key pieces, as people mistype, paste and photograph them. S3 says shares are "held offline" and the server keeps only hashes. The only way an offline share reaches the server at release is typing or pasting it, or a file upload. That is H1's own rejected option A, with a photographable paper share on top. Offline also gives a lone classmate tester no share to hold and nothing to demonstrate. The ceremony is not testable in the 25% usability run.
2. **H1's two-word check is a UI binding, not a security binding.** The server compares two words that the superintendent's screen displays. An attacker with a superintendent session reads them, and so does anyone shoulder-surfing. It needs a second live person, so a lone tester cannot complete it without cheating. It also adds a handoff that H1 itself says custodians will learn to skip. The word check does not stop a replayed approval. A signed nonce and expiry does.

## (c) Concession
Two things I get wrong or underplay. First, server-stored wrapped shares are exposed to **offline brute force of a weak passphrase** if the DB leaks. Offline paper shares do not have that exposure. Second, H1 is right that the approval card must show one request, expire in 15 minutes, never show pieces, and keep the fallback behind a deliberate link. I adopt all of that. Also, `scrypt` is not in WebCrypto, so the browser side uses PBKDF2-SHA256 at 600k iterations or a vetted library, not what P2 wrote.

## (d) Proposed compromise
- **Wrapped shares and signed approval** as above (about 1 day). Passphrases are enforced at enrolment against a short deny list and a minimum length.
- **H1's request card**, with the two-word check **derived from the nonce** and shown on both the custodian's screen and the requester's screen to compare by eye. Nothing is typed. In real mode it is a shared-room check (about 0.5 day).
- **Sandbox mode, labelled "practice":** one tester has a persona switcher that holds all 5 custodians' passphrases pre-set. The ceremony is the same code path, and the screen says plainly that one person is playing several. Real mode refuses two shares from one account (about 0.5 day).
- **Fallback:** a "Something is wrong" link allows 4 of 5 plus a written reason. It is logged and highlighted in the audit view (about 0.25 day).
- **Optional paper export** of one share as a QR code, for S3's offline story. It is not on the critical path (cut if late).

Total about 2.25 days, or about 1.5 days if the ECDSA signature is replaced by an HMAC over the request using a per-custodian secret.

## (e) Biggest risk
All the weight sits in the browser. A classmate who finds one XSS reads the unwrapped share or the private key from memory. We need a strict CSP, no inline script, and a deny test for XSS on every card. A forgotten passphrase also destroys that share, so recovery depends on the 4-of-5 fallback working.
