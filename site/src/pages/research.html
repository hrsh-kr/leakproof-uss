Method

How the remote usability test works

We follow Lecture 3: goals, tasks, data collection, pilot, analysis. Participants complete four tasks on the prototypes with a scenario, a goal, an end line and a two-minute guide. We record, for each task, whether they finished or skipped, how long it took and how many taps they made. After each task they answer a comprehension question with a correct answer, rate the task's difficulty (a 7-point single ease question) and tell us if they got stuck (a critical incident: what they tried, what they expected, what happened).

Before the tasks we ask where they think leaks happen and how they think a paper travels (mental models, Lecture 4), and how acceptable printing at the centre would be. After the tasks we ask a 2-item usability score, trust, acceptance again, and an "explain it to a friend" question that tests whether the idea comes across. Participants can then vote on our five design choices and tell us why, type or speak.

We chose a remote test because we need many people quickly. Its weakness is that nobody watches. So we also plan watched sessions (study E) for depth. Consent is shown first and in plain language; names and contact details are stored apart from answers; we keep spoken answers only as text.

Insights

What the evidence suggests so far

A first SWOT drawn from secondary research. These are hypotheses to test, and they will change as study data arrives.

Strengths

No single person can open the paper. A late draw limits what a leaker knows. Copies are traceable. The log shows tampering.

Weaknesses

Every centre needs a printer, power and a connection. Several people must be reachable. The sealing step has to be trusted. More steps for staff.

Opportunities

Urgency after 2026. A 2024 print-at-centre proposal that was not piloted. Universities as a first place to try it. A 2024 law that holds service providers liable.

Threats

A dishonest quorum. Centres without infrastructure. Pressure on staff and custodians. Private channels we cannot see. Fairness worries about varied order.

Limits

What this research cannot tell you

  • Most participants are classmates and other students, not real paper setters or centre staff. We use them as stand-ins and plan interviews with people closer to the work.
  • The sample is self-selected and small. We report numbers only when 5 or more people answered.
  • A remote test cannot watch what a person does. Timing and tap counts are a weak stand-in for observation.
  • The prototypes simplify the real tool and use simulated data. Understanding the demo is not the same as operating a real system under pressure.
  • Spoken answers are converted to text by the participant's browser, and accuracy varies, especially with accents. Participants can correct the text.
  • Reports on real leaks disagree, and some come from news summaries. We show them as reported.
